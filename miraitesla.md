# Toyota Mirai vs Tesla Model 3 / Gen‑3  
### Power Density • Stack Scaling • Kerb Weight • Executive Ride Quality

This document focuses on the **real engineering differences** between the Toyota Mirai (FCEV) and Tesla Model 3 / Gen‑3 (BEV), specifically:

- Instantaneous **power density**  
- **Stack vs pack** electrochemical output  
- **Kerb weight** and future lightweighting potential  
- **Stack scaling** and why FCEVs can gain power without gaining weight  
- **Executive‑class ride quality** and NVH characteristics  
- A clean **performance & refuel/charge comparison table**

No lifecycle emissions arguments.  
No energy‑per‑km debates.  
Only the engineering realities that matter for performance, refinement, and future capability.

## 1. Core Architecture Differences

### Toyota Mirai (FCEV)
- Fuel cell stack: **113–134 kW**
- Buffer battery: **~1.6–2 kWh**
- Drive motor: **130–150 kW**
- Stack mass: **~56–60 kg**
- Effective stack power density: **~0.3–0.4 kW/kg**
- Ride quality: **exceptional executive‑class NVH**

### Tesla Model 3 / Gen‑3 (BEV)
- Battery pack: **~75 kWh**
- Peak discharge capability: **~200–250 kW**
- Pack mass: **~480–500 kg**
- Effective pack power density: **~2.5–3.0 kW/kg**
- Ride quality: good, but not Mirai‑level isolation

---

## 2. The Real Power Density Difference (Stack vs Pack)

Comparing **electrochemical hardware delivering peak power**:



![Power density ratio](https://latex.codecogs.com/png.latex?\dpi{110}\frac{3.0\ \mathrm{kW/kg\ (BEV\ pack)}}{0.3\ \mathrm{kW/kg\ (Mirai\ stack)}}\approx10\times)


This is the **10× power density gap**:

- BEV packs can dump huge instantaneous current.
- FCEV stacks are limited by gas flow, membrane hydration, and reaction kinetics.

But crucially:

### FCEV stacks scale linearly  
Double the active area → **double the kW**  
Ancillaries (compressor, humidifier, coolant loops) **do not** scale linearly.

This means:

> A future Mirai with a 200–250 kW stack is absolutely feasible  
> **without** doubling system mass.

BEVs cannot do this without adding battery mass, which destroys dynamics.

---

## 3. Kerb Weight: Why Mirai Can Go Lower

Current kerb weights:

- **Mirai Gen‑2:** ~1,850 kg  
- **Model 3 LR:** ~1,760–1,820 kg  

But Mirai has **more room to drop**:

### Weight‑reduction pathways
- Next‑gen carbon‑wrapped hydrogen tanks  
- Thinner stamped bipolar plates  
- Downsized ancillaries once reliability is proven  
- Smaller buffer battery as transient response improves  

A future Mirai could realistically reach:

> **1,700–1,750 kg**  
> while *increasing* power output.

BEVs cannot reduce weight without reducing battery capacity.

This is a **core strength** of FCEVs and not easily matched by BEVs.

## 6. Bottom Line

- Mirai is **under‑developed** in performance terms — intentionally.  
- Toyota could **dramatically increase stack power** without increasing ancillaries.  
- Future FCEVs could have **higher power density and lower kerb weight** than BEVs.  
- BEVs hit a scaling wall: more power → more battery → more weight.  
- FCEVs do **not** hit that wall — stack scaling is cheap once mature.

Hydrogen FCEVs have **real future performance potential** that is not yet exploited.
